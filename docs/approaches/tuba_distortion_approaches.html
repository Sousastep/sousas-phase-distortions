Novel Distortion Approaches for Live Tuba

Working notes on distortion/modulation techniques for a monophonic, pitch-tracked tuba signal. Written with RNBO (Max/MSP) in mind, but everything here is generic DSP and ports to any sample-rate environment.

Already in use (baseline): oversampled atan waveshaping, MS-20-style diode-clipper filter distortion, pitch-tracked ring modulation with a sub-octave modulator, and a "sine-ification" front end (pitch-tracked bell EQ boosting the fundamental by +12 to +48 dB).

The central idea that unlocks most of what follows: once the tuba is reduced to something close to a sine wave, it becomes a phasor — a signal with a well-defined instantaneous phase θ(t) and instantaneous amplitude A(t). Almost every classic abstract synthesis technique (FM/PM, phase distortion, phaseshaping, waveshaping, SSB) is a function of a phasor. So instead of synthesizing a phasor from an oscillator, you extract one from the player and run it through the same machinery. Lazzarini & Timoney call this family "adaptive" synthesis (Adaptive FM, Adaptive Phase Distortion, etc.).

0. The front end: turning the tuba into a phasor

Everything below wants some subset of these signals:

Signal Meaning How to get it
f0(t) tracked fundamental (Hz) pitch tracker (YIN / autocorrelation / fzero~-style), smoothed, with hold-on-silence
s(t) near-sinusoidal tuba bell EQ at f0, +12…48 dB, optionally a steep pitch-tracked band-pass afterward
s_a(t) = s(t) + j·ŝ(t) analytic signal ŝ = Hilbert transform of s (90° phase-shifted copy)
A(t) = sqrt(s² + ŝ²) instantaneous amplitude from the analytic signal
θ(t) = atan2(ŝ, s) instantaneous phase, wrapped [-π, π) from the analytic signal
φ(t) = (θ + π) / 2π normalized phasor [0, 1) rescale θ; this is a "player-driven phasor~"
u(t) = s(t) / (A(t) + ε) unit-amplitude sine dynamics removed; use for modulation
E(t) slow amplitude envelope (RMS/peak) envelope follower; use as a control for drive/index

Notes on the front end:

  • Hilbert transform. Two practical options. (a) A pair of parallel IIR allpass cascades whose outputs differ by ~90° over the audio band (Max's hilbert~ abstraction is built this way; it's a handful of biquads and trivial to rebuild in RNBO/gen). Very low latency, slight phase error at the band edges. (b) An FIR Hilbert filter — accurate but adds N/2 samples of latency, and for a 30–40 Hz fundamental you need a long kernel. For a live instrument, the IIR allpass pair is the right choice.
  • Amplitude normalization is essential. The tuba's dynamics are huge. If you use raw s(t) as a modulator, the modulation index rides on the player's volume and you get chaos on accents and nothing on soft notes. Divide by A(t) (fast, sample-accurate) to get u(t), then re-introduce dynamics deliberately by mapping E(t) to index / drive / mix. This gives you two independent knobs — "how loud" and "how distorted" — that you can re-couple however you like (e.g., quiet = clean, loud = destroyed, or the inverse).
  • Boost vs. bandpass. The bell boost keeps the original harmonics underneath (nice for blending), but the residual harmonics make θ(t) jittery. If a technique needs a really clean phasor, follow the boost with a pitch-tracked 2nd/4th-order band-pass or a resonant low-pass sitting just above f0. Alternatively use a phase-locked oscillator: a sine oscillator whose phase is nudged toward θ(t) each sample (a PLL). This yields a perfectly clean phasor that still follows lip glissandi, vibrato, and pitch bends.
  • Silence / gating. θ(t) becomes noise when A(t) → 0. Freeze f0, fade the wet signal with E(t), and clamp u(t) when A is below a threshold.
  • Unwrapping. For techniques that need a monotonic phase (phaseshaping, hard sync detection) you don't need to unwrap — the wrapped φ ∈ [0,1) is the phasor. But if you want to derive an octave-down phasor (φ/2) you need to count wraps; see §5.

1. Adaptive FM / PM (the tuba as modulator, carrier, or both)

Classic FM: y = cos(ω_c·t + I·cos(ω_m·t)). In the adaptive version, one or both sinusoids are replaced by the tuba.

1a. Tuba as modulator, synthesized carrier

y(t) = A(t) · cos( 2π·(r · f0(t))·t  +  I(t) · u(t) )
  • The carrier is a clean oscillator at a ratio r of the tracked pitch. Integer r gives harmonic spectra; r = 0.5, 1.5, √2… gives sub-octaves, fifths, and inharmonic "bell-brass". Because both sides follow f0, the spectrum's shape stays constant as the player moves — it behaves like a stable timbre, not an effect.
  • I = modulation index. Map E(t) → I so breath pressure controls brightness (the Chowning brass recipe, but with real breath).
  • Because u(t) still contains some residual tuba harmonics, the sidebands are a little "dirty" compared to textbook FM — this is a feature; it sounds like the player, not a DX7.

1b. Tuba as carrier (self-modulation via delay line) — Lazzarini/Timoney "AdFM"

Phase modulation of an arbitrary signal is just a modulated delay line:

y(t) = x(t − d(t)),     d(t) = d0 + (I / (2π·f0(t))) · u(t)
  • Here x can be the full, un-boosted tuba. The result is FM applied to the actual brass spectrum: every harmonic gets its own sideband cluster, producing thick, chorus-y-to-metallic timbres depending on I.
  • Dividing the depth by f0 keeps the effective index constant across register (a fixed delay depth is a much larger phase swing at high pitch).
  • Use interpolated (cubic / allpass) fractional delay; linear interpolation will low-pass and gurgle at large depths.
  • Variation: modulate the delay with cos(2θ), cos(θ/2) etc. (derived from θ, §3) for octave-locked or sub-octave-locked FM.

1c. Feedback FM

Feed the output back into the delay/phase: d(t) = d0 + k·y(t−1). Small k = sawtooth-ish brightening; large k = chaotic, noisy, "torn" brass. Very sensitive; clamp k and keep it oversampled.

1d. Asymmetric / single-sideband FM

Using the analytic signal you can generate only upper or only lower sidebands (Lazzarini & Timoney, "Asymmetric-Spectra Methods for Adaptive FM", DAFx-08; "Split-Sideband Synthesis", ICMC-08). Independent control of upper vs. lower sidebands lets you brighten without adding sub-fundamental mud — often the issue with FM on a bass instrument.

Watch out for: aliasing (FM sidebands extend far; keep the 2–4× oversampling you already use), and pitch-tracker glitches (a wrong octave estimate instantly changes the ratio — slew-limit f0 and reject jumps).

2. Phaseshaping / Adaptive Phase Distortion (the tuba as a phasor)

This is the direct application of the linked SMC 2010 paper (Kleimola, Lazzarini, Timoney, Välimäki — Phaseshaping Oscillator Algorithms for Musical Sound Synthesis) plus Lazzarini et al.'s Adaptive Phase Distortion Synthesis (DAFx-09).

The paper's framing

The paper's central observation is that FM, phase distortion, and waveshaping are all the same thing viewed differently. A sinusoidal oscillator is a waveshaper cos(·) acting on a sawtooth — the phasor φ(n) ∈ [0,1). Phase distortion is therefore double waveshaping:

y = cos( 2π · g(φ) )      φ = phasor (unipolar modulo counter), g = "phaseshaper"

A linear g just transposes; a nonlinear g changes the waveform. The paper then builds a small vocabulary of elementary phaseshapers and nests them (function composition, f ∘ g ∘ h) to reproduce classic oscillator effects and invent new ones — explicitly noting that this modular approach maps directly onto Max/Pd/Reaktor patching.

For the tuba: replace the oscillator's φ(n) with the extracted phasor φ(t) from §0. The player decides where in the cycle we are; g decides what the waveform looks like. Multiply by A(t) to restore dynamics. Everything below is then a live effect rather than an oscillator.

Elementary phaseshapers (paper §2)

Name Definition Role
mod1[x] x − floor(x) (fractional part) wrap back to [0,1)
modm[x, m] x − m·floor(x/m), real m wrap at an arbitrary modulus
gb[x] 2x − 1 unipolar → bipolar
gu[x] 0.5x + 0.5 bipolar → unipolar
glin[x, a1, a0] a1·x + a0 scale/offset; a1 > 1 shortens the phase period, a1 < 1 changes slope
gtri[x, a1] mod1 ∘ glin ∘ abs ∘ gb triangular phase (phase cycle runs twice per period, second half time-reversed)
gpulse[x, w] x(n) − x(n + wP) (difference of two ramps) or trivial 1 if x<w else 0 rectangular signal, width w
gvtri[x, w, a1] mod1 ∘ glin ∘ svtri, svtri = aT·(gb[x]² − gb[x−w]²) + 0.5, aT = 1/(8(w−w²)) variable-slope (tilted) triangle phase
gripple[x, m] x + modm[x, m] (small m) phase ramp with small-amplitude ripples
polyBLEP-mod mod1 whose discontinuity is smoothed by a 2-sample polyBLEP correction anti-aliased wrap ("soft modulo")

The key trick that appears again and again: mod1 ∘ glin with a1 > 1 produces a fractional-period ramp — a phase that completes a1 cycles per fundamental period, then snaps back. That single composition is the engine behind hard sync, supersaw, and the resonant sweeps.

Oscillator algorithms from the paper, re-read as tuba effects (paper §3)

Algorithm Composition (on the tuba phasor φ) What you hear
Waveslice (Minimoog saw) sin(2π · glin[φ, a1=0.25]) only a quarter of a sine per period → analog-style saw; other a1 < 1 slice other portions
Single-oscillator hard sync gb ∘ mod1 ∘ glin[φ, a1], then cos or output the ramp directly a slave oscillator at a1 · f0 hard-synced to the tuba's period, with no second oscillator; sweep a1 for classic sync
Soft sync gb ∘ gtri[φ, a1] (optionally through a triangular waveshaper) slope inverts at the sync point instead of resetting → gentler sync
PWM gb ∘ gpulse[φ, w] or spulse,b ∘ mod1 ∘ glin[φ, a1] pulse with duty w; with a1 > 1 becomes a synced square
Triangle modulation (JP-8000) xT = aTM·gtri[φ,2] − 1; y = 2(xT − ceil(xT) − 0.5), aTM ∈ [0.7, 1] odd harmonics only, 3rd dominant, formant-like sweeps as aTM moves
Supersaw (JP-8000) sin(2π · gb{ modm[ modm[a1φ, m1] + a1φ, m2 ] }), a1 = 1.5, m2 ≈ 0.88, LFO on m1 seven-detuned-saw thickness from one sinusoidal waveshaper; on a tuba this is a huge unison pad locked to the played note
Variable-slope ramp → sine φ · (1 + gpulse[φ − 1, w]), then sin alternating half- and full-cycle sine segments; w moves modest formants across the spectrum
Tilted-triangle → sine sin(2π · gvtri[φ, w, a1]) stronger formant whose position is set by a1; every 4th harmonic missing outside it; a1 → 0 tilts the sine toward a bright quarter-cycle segment (spectral-tilt control)

A few more from the wider PD tradition that fit the same framework:

Phaseshaper g(φ) Result
Casio-CZ knee: fast ramp to 1/2 at φ = d, slow ramp after sine → saw morph; d → 0 gives the CZ "resonant saw"
g = φ + I·sin(2πφ) phase modulation with a phase-locked modulator (FM with zero drift)
g = φ^p asymmetric waveforms, odd/even balance
cos(2π·n·φ) windowed by a saw/triangle of φ CZ "resonance": filter-sweep sound without a filter

Practical points:

  • This is not a resynthesizer in the vocoder sense — it's sample-accurate, essentially zero-latency (limited only by the Hilbert filter), and follows every wobble of θ(t). Residual harmonics in s(t) cause small phase jitter that adds a natural "grain" to the synthesized waveform; a cleaner front end (PLL, §0) removes it if unwanted.
  • The paper's own conclusion is the tuba's selling point. The authors note that these algorithms "respond well to secondary control streams that carry minute articulated expressions of the performer." Here the primary signal already is the performer: φ carries every pitch bend, and E(t) (breath) can drive a1, w, m1, aTM.
  • Aliasing. Anything with a mod1 wrap (hard sync, supersaw, PWM) is discontinuous and aliases. The paper's fix is polyBLEP: add a 2nd-order correction polynomial to the two samples straddling each wrap. You know the phasor, so detecting the wrap is trivial; the paper even proposes treating "polyBLEP-smoothed mod1" as its own phaseshaper entity. The paper notes sinusoidal waveshaping alone (e.g. the waveslice) suppresses aliasing less well than polyBLEP. Oversampling is the brute-force alternative and combines well. Note that with an extracted phasor the phase increment Ts = f0/fs (needed by polyBLEP) comes from your tracker, or from differentiating θ — smooth it.
  • Nesting is the design language. Because every block takes a phase in [0,1) and returns one, you can chain them freely (e.g. gripple → glin → mod1 → gtri → cos). In RNBO this is literally a set of small gen-style abstractions with one inlet/outlet, and new timbres come from re-ordering them.
  • Blending. Cross-fade or sum with the dry tuba. Because the synthesized waveform is phase-locked to the real fundamental, summing produces constructive reinforcement rather than the phasing you get from a free oscillator or a pitch-to-MIDI synth.
  • Sound-design angle: slowly morph g from identity to saw to pulse to synced under envelope control → the tuba "becomes" a synth on crescendos and comes back on decays.

3. Analytic-signal ("instantaneous phase") transformations

With s_a = A·e^(jθ) in hand, several operations become one-liners.

3a. Phase multiplication / harmonic generation (Chebyshev without polynomials)

y_n = A(t) · cos(n · θ(t))

cos(nθ) is exactly the n-th harmonic, phase-locked to the fundamental. Sum a set with weights c_n to draw an arbitrary harmonic spectrum — it's Chebyshev waveshaping (T_n(cos θ) = cos nθ) but computed in the phase domain, so it doesn't degrade when the input isn't a perfect sine. Make c_n depend on E(t) for dynamic brightness. Non-integer n gives inharmonic partials (n = 1.5 = fifth, n = 2.4 = bell).

3b. Phase division → phase-locked sub-octaves (better ring-mod modulators)

Your sub-octave ring mod works well; the usual pain is that a free-running or f0/2 oscillator drifts against the tuba, producing slow beating. Instead derive the modulator from θ itself:

m(t) = cos(θ(t) / 2)      (octave below, perfectly locked)

Because θ/2 needs a consistent unwrapped phase, track wraps: increment a counter on each θ wrap and use (θ_unwrapped) / 2 mod 2π. The result is a modulator that follows every pitch bend and vibrato with zero lag and never beats. cos(θ/3), cos(θ/4) give lower subharmonics; cos(3θ/2) gives a locked fifth. This also doubles as a clean octaver.

3c. Frequency shifting (Bode / SSB)

y = s·cos(ω_s t) − ŝ·sin(ω_s t)       (shift up)    or with `+` (shift down)

Shifts every partial by a fixed Hz, not a ratio → harmonic spectra become inharmonic. Apply to the full tuba (not just the sine): a few Hz gives lush barber-pole phasing; tens of Hz gives clangorous metal; a shift of −f0 folds the fundamental to DC and inverts the lower sidebands ("through-zero" gnarl). Making the shift a ratio of tracked f0 (e.g. −0.5·f0) keeps the type of inharmonicity constant as the player moves.

3d. Phase warping / "phase distortion of the analytic signal"

Reconstruct with a warped phase and the original amplitude:

y = A(t) · cos( θ(t) + h(θ(t)) )

h = I·sin(θ) is PM with a locked modulator; h = I·sin(2θ) adds even harmonics only; h = I·sign(sin θ) gives phase-step distortion (very "digital"); h = I·noise gives phase noise (a breathy, granular blur that stays pitch-centered).

3e. Amplitude/phase decoupling and swapping

Run A(t) and θ(t) through different processing and recombine:

  • Compress or square A(t) while leaving θ alone → distortion that adds no new partials but changes the envelope's shape within each cycle (subtle "tube-y" sag).
  • Hard-clip / quantize A(t) → tremolo-like buzz at the amplitude modulation rate.
  • Use A(t) from the tuba to envelope a different phase source (e.g. θ from a second instrument or a slightly detuned copy) — cross-synthesis.

3f. Rotating ring modulation

Ring mod is y = x·cos(ω_m t). With the analytic signal you can rotate instead of multiply: y = Re{ s_a · e^(jψ(t)) } where ψ is any phase trajectory. With ψ = θ you get A·cos(2θ) (octave up, locked); with ψ = −θ you get A (the envelope, DC); with ψ = θ/2 you get the sub-octave. Sweeping ψ between these continuously morphs through the harmonic series without the intermodulation sidebands that classic RM produces — a smooth "harmonic slider".

4. Waveshaping on the sine-ified signal (with dynamics decoupled)

Your atan and MS-20 stages act on the raw tuba, so harmonic intermodulation dominates (every pair of existing partials spawns sum/difference tones). Feeding them the normalized sine u(t) instead changes the character completely: the output becomes a clean harmonic series with a shape set purely by the transfer function, and the drive is set by you, not the player's volume.

  • Chebyshev / polynomial shaping: y = Σ c_n T_n(u). Deterministic harmonic content; c_n under envelope control. (Equivalent to §3a but cheaper if you don't need the Hilbert branch.)
  • Wavefolding (Buchla 259 / Serge / Lockhart): reflect the signal back when it crosses a threshold. On a near-sine, each additional fold adds a distinctive formant-like bump that sweeps with drive — the classic West-Coast sound, driven by the tuba. Because u is normalized, the "fold count" is a stable parameter; then re-map E(t) to the fold amount to hand control back to the player. Use an anti-derivative anti-aliased (ADAA) or oversampled folder; folding is very alias-prone.
  • Asymmetric / DC-offset shaping: add a bias before the shaper (atan(g·(u + b))) to produce even harmonics; modulate b with cos(θ/2) for octave-locked even/odd flicker.
  • Sync-and-shape: shape u, then multiply by the original tuba. The tuba's own harmonics ring-modulate against the newly created ones, but since both are locked to f0, the result stays harmonic — a way to get "harmonic-series-preserving" ring mod.
  • Parallel-band shaping: split into fundamental (s) and residual (x − s); shape each differently and re-sum. The fundamental branch can be brutal while the residual stays clean, preserving articulation and breath noise.

5. Extensions of pitch-tracked ring modulation

Since RM with a sub-octave already sounds good, some near neighbours:

  • Locked modulators via phase division (§3b) — eliminates drift/beating.
  • Ratio sweeps as gesture: modulator at r·f0 where r slides between "nice" values (0.5 → 0.75 → 1.5). With a locked phasor these are glitch-free.
  • Complex modulators: instead of a sine at f0/2, use a waveform (saw, pulse, or a §2 phaseshaped wave) at f0/2. Each modulator harmonic contributes its own sideband set; a sub-octave saw modulator yields a full "sub-harmonic series" spectrum.
  • AM vs RM: y = x·(1 + k·m) keeps the carrier (original) present; k blends from tremolo-like to full RM.
  • Feedback AM (Kleimola et al.): y[n] = x[n]·(1 + k·y[n−1]). Cheap, stable for k < 1, produces a sawtooth-like brightening whose harmonic slope tracks k. Works directly on the full tuba and is a rich, unusual sound on a bass instrument.
  • Sub-octave via AM squaring: x · cos(θ/2) vs. x · u (i.e. A·cos²θ, octave up + DC). Combined with a high-pass this is a very clean octave-up "harmoniser".

6. Hybrid / meta ideas

  • Hard-sync a real oscillator to the player. Reset a synth oscillator's phase each time φ wraps (or on rising zero-crossings of s). Run the oscillator at r·f0 with r non-integer for classic sync sweeps. This is the same as the frac(nφ) phaseshaper (§2) but works with any oscillator/wavetable you already have.
  • Spectral-envelope transfer (ModFM vocoder idea, Lazzarini & Timoney 2011). Measure the tuba's spectral envelope (a few band followers or an LPC/cepstral fit) and impose it on a phaseshaped/FM synthetic version. The synthetic sound then keeps the tuba's formants and mute/hand-in-bell colour.
  • Chaotic-map distortion locked to θ. Iterate a logistic or tent map once per cycle (triggered on φ wrap) to produce a per-cycle random drive/bias value → "granular" distortion whose grain rate is exactly f0.
  • Multiband adaptive processing. Because you know f0, you can place band splits at 1.5·f0, 3.5·f0, … so each band contains a known set of harmonics; distort each band with a different technique. The bands slide with the pitch, so the treatment per harmonic is constant across the range.

7. General implementation checklist

  1. Oversample any nonlinearity (FM/PM delay mod, folders, shapers, phase distortion). 2× minimum, 4× for folders and high-index FM; half-band FIR or a good elliptic IIR for decimation.
  2. Normalize before modulating (u = s / (A + ε)) and re-map dynamics deliberately (E(t) → index/drive/mix/ratio).
  3. Condition f0: median filter the tracker, reject octave jumps, slew-limit, hold on silence, and derive all tuned parameters (ratios, delays, band edges) from the conditioned value.
  4. Use the phasor, not the frequency, wherever possible. Anything derived from θ(t) is locked to the player and can't drift; anything derived from f0 + a separate oscillator can.
  5. PolyBLEP or oversample discontinuous phaseshapers (saw, sync, pulse).
  6. Gate the wet path with E(t) and clamp θ-derived signals below a level threshold.
  7. Keep a dry/parallel path. Most of these are most musical when summed with, not replacing, the tuba; because they're phase-locked, summing doesn't cause phasing.
  8. Watch DC. Frequency shifting toward 0 Hz, cos², and asymmetric shaping produce DC; high-pass the output (a few Hz, or at ~0.5·f0 tracked).
